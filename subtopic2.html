02 / Bleeding

Pressure can matter.

Recognize serious external bleeding and focus on the first response.

01 / Recognize an emergency

Severe bleeding needs pressure and help.

Blood flowing continuously, spurting, or collecting in a large amount can be life-threatening. Do not wait for the person to become faint. Call emergency services and begin firm pressure immediately.

Protect yourself

Check for danger, get consent when possible, and use disposable gloves or other suitable protection if available.

Press firmly

Place a clean dressing or cloth over the wound. Use firm, steady pressure and keep it there while help is coming.

Keep caring

Watch breathing and responsiveness, reassure the person, and follow dispatcher instructions. A trained helper can use a tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding.

Object in the wound? Leave it in place. Apply pressure around the object rather than pressing onto it.

Source: American Red Cross: Life-Threatening Bleeding.

02 / Everyday wound care

Small cuts and grazes

A shallow cut that stops bleeding can often be cleaned and covered. A deep wound, bleeding that will not stop, loss of feeling, or difficulty moving the affected area needs urgent medical care.

  1. Wash and dry your hands; wear disposable gloves if available.
  2. Control the bleeding with a clean cloth or dressing.
  3. Once a small wound has stopped bleeding, rinse it with clean tap or bottled water.
  4. Clean the surrounding skin with soap and water, then gently dry the area.
  5. Cover with a sterile plaster or dressing. Keep it clean and dry, and replace it when needed.

When should a clinician check it?

Get medical advice for a wound that remains dirty, a bite, or increasing redness, swelling, pain, pus, fever, or feeling unwell. Large or deep cuts and wounds with an embedded object need prompt assessment. Tell the clinician if your tetanus protection may be out of date.

Source: NHS: Cuts and grazes.

03 / Watch the whole person

Shock is a medical emergency.

Medical shock means that the body is not supplying enough oxygen-rich blood to its organs. Severe blood loss is one possible cause. It is different from simply feeling frightened.

Possible signs

Cool, pale, grey, or moist skin; fast breathing; a rapid weak heartbeat; confusion or restlessness; nausea; and excessive thirst.

What to do

Call emergency services, control external bleeding, help the person rest, and protect them from becoming too cold or hot.

Keep checking

Stay with them and monitor breathing and responsiveness. Do not give food or drink. Follow the dispatcher’s advice about positioning and any change in condition.

Source: American Red Cross: Shock.

04 / A common situation

Helping with a nosebleed

Have the person sit upright and lean slightly forward. Pinch the soft part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes and ask them to breathe through their mouth. Leaning forward helps keep blood from running down the throat.

Do not tilt the head back. Seek urgent help if bleeding is heavy, the person becomes faint or has trouble breathing, or it does not stop after 15 minutes of pressure. Get medical advice after a significant head or nose injury.

Source: American Red Cross: Nosebleeds.

05 / Quick answers

Common questions

Should I clean a heavily bleeding wound first?

Controlling life-threatening bleeding and calling for help take priority. Cleaning is for a small wound after bleeding is controlled.

Should I remove glass or another embedded object?

No. Removing it can worsen bleeding. Keep it still, press around it, and get professional help.

What if I am not sure how serious it is?

Describe the bleeding and the person’s condition to emergency services. Continuous or spurting bleeding, faintness, confusion, or abnormal breathing warrants an urgent response.
